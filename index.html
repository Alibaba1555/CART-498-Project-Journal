01

Foundation

Design Pillars

01

Low-Friction Interaction

Performers should be able to publish with very few actions while setting up their equipment.

02

Clear Performance Status

The interface should clearly show whether a performance is being configured, live, paused, or finished.

03

Glanceable Information

Remaining time and live status should be understandable during a quick look at the screen.

04

Flexible Time Management

Performers should be able to extend, pause, resume, or end a session without repeating setup.

02

Primary, Secondary & Future Users

User Personas

01 / 03

A

Primary Persona · Spontaneous Performer

Alex, 24

Student · Guitar and vocals · Montreal

“I want people nearby to know that I’m playing, but I don’t want another complicated setup process.”

Context

Alex performs spontaneously when the location, weather, and available time feel right. The app may be used outdoors, while standing, or with one hand.

Goals

  • Publish a performance in under one minute.
  • Show the correct location and duration.
  • Extend or end the session quickly.

Frustrations

  • Long forms while preparing equipment.
  • Inaccurate map pins.
  • Expired performances remaining live.
Design implication

Prioritize a short setup flow, large controls, and clear confirmation that the performance is live.

M

Secondary Persona · Experienced Busker

Maya, 31

Independent musician · Violin and loop station · Montreal

“My location can change at the last minute. Updating the audience should take seconds.”

Context

Maya performs three or four times per week and has a small local following. Weather, noise, crowds, or local restrictions may force her to move.

Goals

  • Reach followers and nearby listeners.
  • Correct the location when plans change.
  • Extend a successful performance quickly.

Frustrations

  • Repeating the same details every session.
  • Posting updates across several platforms.
  • People arriving after the set has ended.
Design implication

Save reusable profile details and keep location editing, time extension, and End Performance directly accessible.

S

Future Persona · Nearby Listener

Sofia, 22

University student · Music enthusiast · Montreal

“Before I start walking, I need to know the performance is still happening.”

Context

Sofia enjoys discovering local music while walking downtown but rarely knows when or where spontaneous performances are happening.

Goals

  • Find performances within walking distance.
  • See distance and remaining time quickly.
  • Follow musicians she discovers.

Frustrations

  • Outdated or inaccurate event information.
  • Walking to a performance that has ended.
  • Receiving too many notifications.
Design implication

Show accurate distance, time, and live status; update changes immediately and remove expired performance pins.

Research status: These personas represent initial behavioral assumptions and will be revised after user testing.

User Assumptions

Research status: These are initial assumptions. They have not yet been validated through user testing.
  • 01Performers want to publish their current location as quickly as possible.

05

Task Structure

Core User Flow

  1. 01
    Enter Performer Mode

    The main navigation action becomes “Start a Performance.”

  2. 02
    Confirm Location

    The current prototype uses a simulated location. Live location detection remains a future implementation.

  3. 03
    Select Duration

    The performer chooses 30, 45, or 60 minutes.

  4. 04
    Review and Go Live

    The location and duration are confirmed before publication.

  5. 05
    Manage the Session

    The live screen shows remaining time with a double-tap gesture to add 10 minutes and controls to manage the session.

  6. 06
    End Automatically or Manually

    The public pin is removed when the performer ends the session or the timer reaches zero.

Prototype at a Glance

The current version connects finding a nearby set with listening, requesting an encore, saving a ticket, and supporting the musician. This overview describes the latest prototype; the weekly entries keep the earlier decisions and screenshots.

Explore the neighbourhood

A grey-and-white 3D map with varied buildings, two parks, trees, and an animated fountain. Listeners can filter nearby sets and simulate walking toward a performance.

Listen & take part

Alex’s card places the 15-second preview above the main actions. Nearby listeners can simulate a tip, request an encore, and save a Moment Ticket.

Manage a live set

Double-tap the timer to add 10 minutes. Its waves react to the gesture. Below it, the performer reviews one pending encore request at a time and can open the request history.

See support arrive

A successful demo tip to Alex updates the performer’s total and deals a new playing card. The latest amount appears on the card; the running total stays below it.

This is a browser prototype: walking, location, and payments are simulated. Tip updates occur within the same running prototype, not across devices. See the latest screenshots and iteration notes.

UI

Design study / Not implemented in the app

Finding a Musical Character

The feedback on Weeks 01–06 described the UI as too tame and closer to a B2B platform. I can see that in the repeated white panels and restrained typography. I still like the calm base, so I am comparing how much colour and rhythm I can introduce through the same artist-card component.

What I would keep

All three keep straight corners, the same information order, and one clear primary action. I am leaning toward trying the warmer version on the artist card first. The map can stay quieter, while the artist name, preview, and live status carry more personality. These are new visual studies for the journal; I have not tested or applied these colour variations to the prototype yet.

Motion with a purpose

The timer reaction and dealt tip cards are already implemented. They make an action feel acknowledged. I want to use that kind of response selectively rather than making every component move all the time.

Week 03

Building the First Prototype

Building the First Prototype

This week, I created an initial prototype of Corner Set, my platform for connecting street performers with nearby audiences. I focused mainly on the performer side, exploring how someone could start a set and manage their performance time through a simple interface.

Performer Flow

The current flow moves from a home screen to a setup page, where the performer can choose a duration of 30, 45, or 60 minutes. During the set, a large circular timer displays the remaining time, with options to add another 15 minutes or end the performance. I wanted the main information and controls to be easy to read at a glance, since performers should be able to focus on their music without constantly interacting with their phones.

Visual Direction & Prototype States

Visually, I explored a light background with soft blue and green accents. The timer has an organic shape around it, which introduces a sense of movement into an otherwise minimal interface. I also included an offline simulation to explore how the app communicates connection problems. At this stage, the map is illustrative, publishing and syncing are simulated, and the tip jar is only a placeholder.

Reflection

Working on this prototype helped me think more carefully about the different states of a performance: preparing, going live, extending the session, and finishing. It also raised a question about location: the current design shows an approximate public area, but I still need to consider whether that gives audiences enough information to find a performer.

Next Steps — 3D Map & Audience Experience

Next, I would like to experiment with a 3D map and develop the audience side of the platform. Possible features include finding nearby performances, viewing performer profiles, and checking how much time remains in a set. I want to explore whether a 3D view makes discovering performances more intuitive while keeping the interface easy to navigate. My next step is to connect these audience interactions with the performer flow and see how the two experiences work together.

Week 04

Day & Night Modes and the Audience Experience

Responding to Client Requirements

This week, I expanded the Corner Set prototype based on the client's requirements. I added day and night display modes, developed the audience side, and added a tipping page. These updates connect the performer flow from last week with the experience of someone discovering and supporting a nearby performance.

Day & Night Modes

I added an appearance toggle in the profile page so users can switch between a light daytime view and a darker nighttime view. Both modes keep the same layout and controls, while the background, text, and accent colours change. My aim is to make the interface suitable for different lighting conditions without making users relearn how to navigate it. I still need to test readability outdoors and after dark.

Audience Experience & 3D Map

I also added an Audience mode, which users can access by double-tapping the profile avatar. The map uses grey and white 3D buildings, a blue marker for the listener's position, and red signals for nearby performances. The expanding red rings indicate approximate performance areas. The audience page shows the performer's information, distance, and remaining performance time, helping listeners decide which set to approach.

Simulating a Walk & Automatic Connection

The prototype can now simulate an audience member walking toward a performer. Using the testing controls, I can select a performance and move the listener's position closer to it. When the listener enters the nearby area, the interface automatically connects to that performance. This lets me explore the transition from discovering music on the map to viewing the performance's details without asking the listener to manually join. The movement and connection use demo positions rather than live GPS or audio streaming.

Tipping Flow

I added a tipping page so the audience experience can continue from finding a performance to supporting the musician. The audience card includes a “Leave a tip” action, which becomes available after connecting nearby. The payment interaction is simulated at this stage; the focus is on the steps and feedback that make supporting a performer understandable.

Week 05

Finding Music & Supporting Musicians

Working with the New Requests

For this update, I focused on what an audience member needs before choosing a performance: how far away it is, what it sounds like, and who is playing. The client also wanted a quicker way to tip. I built on the existing map and performance cards so these additions would fit into the same browsing experience.

A Smaller Distance Filter

I first tried separate distance buttons, then changed them to a compact slider above the map. The range now runs from 50 to 300 metres, with All at the end. I reduced the upper limit from 500 metres because the demo performances are closer together. The slider takes up less space, although I still need to check whether people notice the All option.

I also added Nina near Leo to explore what happens when two musicians perform close together. When zoomed out, their markers combine into a red dot labelled 2. Clicking it zooms in and reveals the separate markers. This gives me a small example of how to keep a busy map readable.

Giving the Musicians More Personality

The performance cards now include genre tags, a short Behind the Sound introduction, and an Instagram link. I wanted the bios to sound like something a person would actually write. Mika uses lots of emoji, Leo is more casual, and Alex's introduction is simply about liking peanut butter sandwiches.

Hearing a Set Before Walking Over

I added a 15-second preview to Alex's card, using a prerecorded file that starts at the 11-second mark. The player includes play, pause, and a progress bar. This responds to the developer's concern about live audio streaming: a short stored clip is enough to demonstrate how someone could hear a sample before deciding to go. For now, Alex is the only musician with audio, and the sample is test content.

Trying a Faster Tipping Flow

I expanded the tipping popup with an Apple Pay-style screen. After choosing an amount, the listener sees a demo card and double-taps the confirmation area. A simulated Face ID animation leads to a success message. I wanted the interaction to give clear feedback without taking the listener away from the performance. It does not use the camera, authenticate a face, or charge money.

Visual Alerts & Small Fixes

I kept the expanding red rings as the prototype's visual alert for nearby performances. They draw attention to activity while someone is looking at the map. This is my current interpretation of the arrival-alert idea; an opt-in notification when a new set begins nearby is still outside this version.

I also fixed broken characters in the payment screen and moved the desktop project title to the top left. It previously shifted vertically when I switched roles because the audience page was longer than the performer page.

What I Want to Check Next

The cards now give listeners more information, but they also take up more space. I want to see whether someone can find the preview and tipping action without being guided. I also want to check whether the grouped marker reads as two performances, and whether the payment confirmation is clear on a phone. These are questions for testing, rather than results I can claim yet.

Retrospective entry / Added October 26, 2026

Week 06

Save a moment / Stay for another song

I wanted to give listeners something to do once they had found a performance they liked.

Most of my earlier work was about getting people to a performance: finding it on the map, checking the time, and walking closer. For this entry, I focused on what happens after that. I worked on saving a moment, asking for an encore, and making the tipping interaction fit alongside those options.

A small thing to keep

I added a Moment Ticket that listeners can save when they are near a performance. It includes the artist, place, date, and time, with a small “I WAS HERE” stamp. I liked the idea of having something to look back at after coming across music by chance. It is a keepsake, not an admission ticket.

I also gave it a printing animation. The ticket comes out of a slot, which makes saving it feel a little more physical than just pressing a button and seeing a message. Saved tickets appear in My Moments, where they can be opened again or deleted. I kept the collection simple, without adding downloads or a sharing flow.

A little reminder of being there.

“One more song?”

The encore feature lets a nearby listener ask for 1–30 extra minutes and type in a song they would like to hear. I kept the song field optional because someone might simply want the musician to keep playing.

On the performer side, Alex can see the requested time and song, then accept or decline. The timer only increases after acceptance. I felt this was important: enjoying a set does not mean the audience gets to decide how long the musician stays. For now, I kept the feature on Alex’s side so I could work through one complete interaction. The requests are cleared when the set ends.

Keeping tips separate

I kept Leave a tip beside the encore action, building on the payment flow from the previous entry. The Apple Pay-style screen and simulated Face ID give the interaction a clear finish. No actual payment takes place. I also kept tipping separate from requesting a song or saving a ticket. I do not want those actions to feel like something the listener has to pay to unlock.

Making it easier to try

While going back through the prototype, I added a short checklist beside the app so someone can try both roles without me explaining every step. I also connected Alex’s map listing to the live session: the set has to be published before it appears for the audience.

What I still want to find out

The audience card now has several things to do, and I am not sure yet whether all of them need to be visible at once. I want to ask classmates whether Save this moment makes sense before they open the ticket, and whether the encore request feels easy to use. I also want to see if they notice that a request is still waiting for the performer’s answer.

November 3, 2026

Week 07

Refining the Map & Live Interactions

Looking Back at the Review

The review of my work through Week 06 received 14.5/15. The testing controls and focused survey questions worked well, and the interactive journal was also a strength. The main criticism was that the app looked too restrained for live music. That gives me something more specific to work on: making the interface feel musical while keeping it readable. I added three visual directions above to start comparing that balance.

The survey also needs clearer divisions. For the next round, I plan to group it into finding a performance, interacting as a listener, and managing a set, with the open comments at the end. Click counts and navigation paths could help later, but I have not added tracking. The teacher described that as optional, so my priority is the interface exploration and a clearer testing session.

Keeping the Part I Already Liked

This week, I spent most of my time refining the prototype. I already liked the quiet colours and the overall layout, so I wanted to improve it without losing that feeling. The client feedback about bringing the map into the app's design system gave me a starting point. I worked on the buildings first, then moved through the artist cards and the live performance screen.

The Map Got Too Crowded

My first idea was to fill the grey blocks with more buildings and keep the white roads clear. It made the neighbourhood feel more complete, but I went too far. With so many similar tall buildings, I could barely see what was happening between them. I started thinking about the lower buildings around Chinatown and used more variation in height, width, and roof details. This is still an illustrative neighbourhood, not a reconstruction of Place des Arts btw.

I added two parks to open up the view and moved Mika's performance into one of them. At first the parks looked almost identical, so I gave the front one a fountain, added trees, and refined the benches. The fountain now has a small water animation. I also corrected buildings that appeared to overlap. After trying a closer initial camera view, I went back to the earlier angle because it gave a better overview. Adding more detail only helped when I could still read the map.

Making the Interface Feel Consistent

I adjusted the font sizes and weights so names, headings, and supporting text are easier to tell apart. I also gave the buttons clearer priorities and removed the small decorative arrows. I tried rounded cards, but they didn't feel right with the rest of the interface, so I returned the cards, buttons, and nearby artist list to straight corners. I kept the map markers and timer circular because those shapes have a different role.

On the artist card, I brought the existing 15-second preview out of the expandable profile area and made it more visible, with a small animated sound indicator. The biography and genre tags are still available below. I wanted someone to get a sense of the music before reading a whole profile.

Giving the Timer a Response

I removed the large ‘This is your moment.’ heading to give the live screen more space. I also replaced the +15-minute button with a double-tap on the timer that adds 10 minutes. When I tried clicking it, the numbers could become highlighted in blue, which interrupted the effect, so I made that text unselectable. The surrounding waves now react to a tap, and the inner circle briefly changes shape before settling back. I like that the timer responds, although I still need to check whether someone new to the app would discover the gesture.

Turning Tips into Dealt Cards

The tip jar went through a few versions. I wanted receiving a tip to feel noticeable, and the idea of dealing a playing card seemed more interesting than just changing a number. The first flip felt too flat, so I changed it into a card moving in, turning over, and landing slightly out of line with the cards underneath. Leaving the old corners visible makes it feel more like a small pile of cards.

I changed the wording to ‘Tips received’ and made the new amount larger. I originally showed the total on the card too, but it was repetitive, so I moved the running total to a fixed footer with a serif number style that fits the playing-card idea. Successful demo tips from the audience side now update Alex's jar in the same prototype. I also added a +$5 test button to the desktop controls so I can replay the effect easily.

Keeping Requests Under Control

I placed Encore requests and the tip jar next to each other below the timer. Previously, every request added another block of text, which made the left side keep growing. I changed it to show one pending request at a time, with previous and next controls, a position counter, and a separate History view. The performer can still accept or decline a request. With the total at the bottom of the tip panel, the two panels now line up more neatly.

What Still Needs Work

A lot of this week was trying something, seeing that it was too much, and pulling it back. The buildings, card animation, and typography all needed that. I am happier with how the prototype looks now, but I haven't tested these changes with other people yet. Next I want to check the double-tap gesture, whether the request controls are obvious, and whether the two panels remain readable on a phone. The client also asked for less separation between audience and performer accounts; the current role switch is still there, so that part needs more work. I also noticed the screenshot checklist still says +15 MIN, and that needs to match the new timer interaction.
